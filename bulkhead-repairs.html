The Importance of Bulkheads

Your Bulkheads Are Part
of the Boat’s Structure.

Bulkheads help divide and reinforce the hull, support loads, and maintain your boat’s shape. Over time, they can be affected by water intrusion, rot, delamination, impact, stress cracks, or failed bonding. Our team repairs and replaces damaged bulkheads with high-quality materials to restore strength and reliability.

Captain Dave’s Rule

An Offshore Boat Should Have
at Least Four Watertight Bulkheads.

Bulkheads are the transverse structure of the hull: they add athwartships strength and cut down flexing. On a boat that goes offshore they do a second job, dividing the hull into compartments so that one breach does not flood the whole boat. In Captain Dave’s view that takes at least four, and each one guards against a different failure. The bottom line is survivability.

What wears a bulkhead out
  • Flexing as the hull works through a seaway
  • Vibration from the main engines and generator
  • Moisture standing in the bilge
  • Heat from the engine room
  1. 1

    Collision bulkhead

    Forward, in the bow

    The compartment ahead of it is usually the anchor-rode locker. In a head-on collision, if the collision bulkhead holds, you are still afloat.

  2. 2

    Forward engine-room bulkhead

    Ahead of the main engines

    Together with the aft bulkhead it closes off the engine room, which holds the stuffing boxes and the through-hull fittings below the waterline. If one of them leaks, or the hull is breached there, the water stays in the engine room.

  3. 3

    Aft engine-room bulkhead

    Behind the main engines

    Completes the engine compartment and doubles as a sound, heat and fire barrier between the machinery and the rest of the boat.

  4. 4

    Lazarette bulkhead

    Aft, around the rudders

    Closes off the lazarette where the rudder posts come through the hull. Dropping a rudder offshore is a bad day even for a seasoned skipper; the bulkhead keeps it from becoming a sinking.

Construction & Tabbing

How Bulkheads Are Built
and Tabbed Into the Hull.

Until the late 1990s most production builders made bulkheads from marine plywood and bonded them to the hull with polyester resin and fiberglass: 1.5 oz chopped-strand mat and 24 oz woven roving, cut into strips eight to twelve inches wide. The strips are wetted out and laminated across the joint, part on the hull and part on the plywood, so the bulkhead and the hull become one structure. Builders call this taping-in, or tabbing.

Done correctly, a tabbed joint is strong. But over years of flexing, vibration and bilge moisture it is not uncommon to find the fiberglass delaminating away from the plywood, or the plywood itself degrading behind a skin that still looks fine. Neither shows from across the bilge; a close inspection tells the tale.

  • Marine plywoodThe usual core before the late 1990s
  • 1.5 oz mat + 24 oz woven rovingThe factory tabbing laminate
  • 8–12″ stripsLaminated onto both the hull and the bulkhead
  • Polyester resinThe original production bonding resin
